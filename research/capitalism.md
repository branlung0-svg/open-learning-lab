## 2. Sourced numbers (first batch)

**Extreme poverty today.** The World Bank raised its extreme-poverty line from $2.15 to $3.00 a day (2021 prices) in June 2025; by that line about 838 million people were extremely poor in 2022, an upward revision of roughly 125 million that reflects the new line, not a poorer world. Its 2025 estimate is about 831 million, down from about 2.3 billion in 1990. Most of the extreme poor now live in Sub-Saharan Africa and in fragile or conflict-hit countries. Sources: [World Bank factsheet, June 2025](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/factsheet/2025/06/05/june-2025-update-to-global-poverty-lines); [World Bank poverty overview](https://www.worldbank.org/en/topic/poverty/overview). Confidence: **Strong** (survey-based; the line itself is a choice).

**Extreme poverty in 1820.** Bourguignon and Morrisson (2002) estimated about 84% of the world below $1 a day (1985 prices) in 1820. Michail Moatsos's newer reconstruction gives about three-quarters. Sources: [Gapminder documentation](https://gapminder.org/data/documentation/epovrate); [Our World in Data, Extreme poverty in brief](https://ourworldindata.org/extreme-poverty-in-brief). Confidence: **Contested** (see Debate A).

**Wealth concentration (2025).** World Inequality Report 2026: the top 10% hold about three-quarters of global wealth, the bottom half about 2%, the top 1% about 37%. The top 0.001% (under 60,000 people) went from about 4% of global wealth in 1995 to over 6% in 2025. Source: [WIR 2026 technical report](https://wir2026.wid.world/www-site/uploads/2025/12/WIR26_TechnicalReport.pdf). Confidence: **Strong** for direction; levels depend on WID's methods, which critics (e.g., Auten and Splinter for the US) dispute.

**Great Depression (USA).** Unemployment went from 3.2% in 1929 to 24.9% in 1933 in Lebergott's series (the one BLS uses). Darby's series, which counts work-relief jobs as employed, peaks lower. Sources: [Lebergott, NBER 1957](https://www.nber.org/chapters/c2644.pdf); [Margo, Journal of Economic Perspectives 1993](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/title/journal-economic-perspectives-1167/employment-unemployment-1930s-2391/fulltext). Confidence: **Established**.

**Post-Soviet mortality crisis.** Russian male life expectancy fell from 64.2 in 1989 to 57.6 in 1994, one of the fastest peacetime falls ever recorded; neighbors fell 3–5 years. Alcohol is the agreed proximate cause; the deeper cause is debated (Debate D). Sources: [Brainerd & Cutler, JEP 2005](https://web.williams.edu/Economics/brainerd/papers/jep05.pdf); [Leon et al., Lancet 1997](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0140-6736(97)03360-6). Confidence: **Established** (the fall), **Contested** (the cause).

**Chile.** Poverty was about 45% in 1987 and 8.6% in 2017 (national CASEN line). The Gini fell from about 52 (1990) to about 44 (2017) yet stayed among the OECD's highest; the 1982–83 crash cut output by about 19%; inflation hit 375% in 1974. Sources: [World Bank Chile poverty brief 2021](https://databank.worldbank.org/data/download/poverty/987B9C90-CB9F-4D93-AE8C-750588BF00QA/SM2021/Global_POVEQ_CHL.pdf); [Cato Journal, "The Fall of Chile", 2020](https://www.cato.org/cato-journal/fall-2020/fall-chile); critic: [Dollars & Sense](https://www.dollarsandsense.org/is-chile-a-neoliberal-success/). The dictatorship's repression must be stated alongside: Rettig and Valech commissions. Confidence: **Strong**.

**South vs North Korea.** Bank of Korea: North Korea's 2024 per-capita GNI was 1.72 million won, about 1/29 of the South's 50.1 million won. Source: [KEIA summary of BoK estimate](https://keia.org/the-peninsula/what-to-make-of-north-koreas-fastest-economic-growth-since-2016/). Confidence: **Strong** (BoK estimates the North indirectly). Note: the South's takeoff came under a state-directed developmental model and military rule, not laissez-faire, which the page must say.

**Bengal famine 1943.** Scholarly consensus about 2.1 million deaths (range 0.8–3.8 million; Sen 2.7–3 million for 1943–46). Sen's entitlement explanation (inflation and wartime policy, not a lack of food) is the mainstream view; Tauger and others stress crop failure. Source: [Ó Gráda, UCD working paper 2010](https://www.ucd.ie/economics/t4media/WP10_21.pdf). Confidence: **Strong** (toll), **Contested** (weighting of causes and of Churchill's role).

**The word.** "Capitalism" in the systemic sense: Louis Blanc, 1850; Proudhon used it in the 1860s; first English use Thackeray, *The Newcomes* (1854); established as an analytical term by Werner Sombart, *Der moderne Kapitalismus* (1902), and Weber (1905). Marx wrote of "capital" and the "capitalist mode of production" and almost never used the noun "capitalism". Confidence: **Strong**. Source needed beyond secondary: OED entry; Braudel, *Wheels of Commerce* (1979), ch. 3.

## 3. Debates to present with both sides at full strength

**A. Did capitalism end mass poverty, or did it create modern poverty first?**
- *Yes:* Roser/OWID, Pinker, McCloskey, Deaton. The share in extreme poverty fell from the large majority in 1820 to about 10% today while population grew sevenfold; the timing follows industrialization and trade.
- *Not so simple:* Hickel and Sullivan (World Development, 2023), drawing on Robert Allen: GDP-based 1820 estimates miss subsistence and commons; Allen's consumption data show low extreme poverty in Britain and the US around 1800, and India's rising from about 23% (1810) to over 50% under colonial rule. They estimate 50–165 million excess deaths in India 1880–1920, depending on the assumed baseline.
- *Critiques of the critique:* Rutar (2024) and others: many colonial empires (Spain, Portugal) were pre-capitalist; the 165 million figure rests on an English 16th–17th century death rate as India's baseline; excess-mortality methods are sensitive to baselines. Roser and Noah Smith: poverty fell under any reasonable line after 1950.
- Sources: [Hickel 2021](https://www.jasonhickel.org/blog/2021/3/28/extreme-poverty-isnt-natural-it-is-created); [OWID](https://ourworldindata.org/extreme-poverty-in-brief). To fetch: Sullivan & Hickel 2023, *World Development* 161; Allen 2017 "Absolute poverty: when necessity displaces desire", *AER*; Rutar 2024.

**B. Was slavery central to the rise of American capitalism?**
- *Central:* Baptist, *The Half Has Never Been Told* (2014); Beckert, *Empire of Cotton* (2014); the "New History of Capitalism". Cotton was over half of US exports; forced migration of about a million enslaved people to the cotton frontier.
- *Overstated:* Olmstead and Rhode (2018, *Explorations in Economic History*): the 4× rise in pounds picked per day (1800–1860) came mainly from new cotton varieties, not a "whipping machine"; Baptist's claim that cotton was nearly half of US economic activity double-counts. Reviewers in the *Journal of Economic History* (2015) roundtable were severe.
- *What both sides accept:* slavery was brutal, profitable, financed through national and global markets, and cotton fed the industrial revolution's leading sector.
- Sources: [Olmstead & Rhode 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eeh.2017.12.002); [Chronicle of Higher Education, "Shackles and Dollars"](https://www.chronicle.com/article/ShacklesDollars/238598).

**C. Is inequality rising, and does it matter?** Piketty and the World Inequality Lab vs critics of the tax-data methods (Auten & Splinter; Geloso et al.). Global inequality between countries has *fallen* since about 1990 (China, India), while inequality within many countries has risen. Both are true at once.

**D. What caused Russia's 1990s death spike?** Stuckler, King & McKee (Lancet 2009): rapid mass privatization. Earle & Gehlbach and Gerry et al. (Lancet 2010): the link is not robust when the privatization measure is recomputed. Bhattacharya et al.: the end of Gorbachev's anti-alcohol campaign explains much of it. Brainerd: stress and economic collapse matter. Sources: [Earle & Gehlbach 2010](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(10)60159-6); [Bhattacharya, Gathmann & Miller 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3818525).

**E. Chile: miracle or warning?** Growth and poverty reduction were real and continued under democratic governments; so were extreme inequality, privatized pensions with low payouts, and the 2019 protests. The reforms were imposed by a dictatorship that killed and tortured thousands: the economic record and the human-rights record are both part of the case.

**F. Do markets work for healthcare, housing and climate?** Externalities and public goods (Pigou, Samuelson, Stern Review, Nordhaus) vs government failure (Buchanan, public choice). Present carbon pricing, cap-and-trade results and their limits.

**G. Is China capitalist?** Shared with the Marxism dive (debate already there): link, don't duplicate.

## 4. True or false candidates (need a source each)

- "Adam Smith's main idea was the invisible hand." Mostly false: the phrase appears once in *The Wealth of Nations* (Book IV, ch. 2) and once in *Theory of Moral Sentiments*. Source: Smith texts on Wikisource/Online Library of Liberty.
- "Marx coined the word capitalism." False (see section 2).
- "The Nordic countries are socialist." Mostly false by the textbook definition: private ownership and markets, plus high taxes and a large welfare state. Danish PM Rasmussen said so at Harvard in 2015. Source to fetch.
- "Most new businesses fail in their first year." False: BLS Business Employment Dynamics show about 20% of new establishments close within one year, about half within five. Source to fetch: bls.gov/bdm.
- "The 2008 crash was caused by deregulation alone." Mixed: the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission majority vs dissents. Source to fetch.
- "Free trade makes everyone better off." Partial: gains on average are well established; the "China shock" research (Autor, Dorn, Hanson 2013) shows concentrated, lasting local losses.
